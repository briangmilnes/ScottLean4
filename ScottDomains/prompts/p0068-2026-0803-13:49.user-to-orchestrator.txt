## The one tactic that matters here: `ring`

`ring` is a **decision procedure** for equalities in any commutative ring. It normalizes *both* sides to a canonical polynomial form and checks they're identical. So a factorization proof is just:

```lean
example : x^2 - y^2 = (x - y) * (x + y) := by ring
```

You *state* the factored form; `ring` multiplies out `(x−y)(x+y)`, gets `x² − y²`, sees the two sides match, and closes the goal. Put your cursor right after `by ring` and the Infoview says **`No goals`** — the kernel has accepted it. Move the cursor *before* `ring` and you'll see the goal it's about to discharge.

The degree-3 forms (`x³ − y³`, `x³ + y³`) and the ternary form (`x·y − x·z = x·(y − z)`) all close the same way — `ring` doesn't care about the degree.

## Factoring framed as divisibility

To say *"(x − y) is a factor of x² − y²"* is `(x - y) ∣ (x² - y²)`, which unfolds to `∃ c, x² − y² = (x − y)·c`. You supply the **cofactor** as the witness, and `ring` proves the equation — the *entire proof is a term*:

```lean
example : (x - y) ∣ (x^2 - y^2) := ⟨x + y, by ring⟩
```

That `⟨x + y, by ring⟩` is the factorization made explicit: the divisor is `x − y`, the quotient is `x + y`.

## `ring_nf` — watch it multiply out

Where `ring` *proves* a stated equality, `ring_nf` **normalizes** an expression in place. Cursor after the `ring_nf` line and the Infoview shows `(x − y)(x + y)` expanded to its canonical `x² − y²`.

## The honest point — verify, not discover

This is the key thing, and it's the opposite of Mathematica's `Factor[]`: **Lean has no built-in tactic that *finds* a factorization for you.** `ring` *checks* the factors you propose. To *discover* them you'd use a CAS (or your own head) and bring the answer to Lean. Formal factorization *theory* — irreducibility, unique factorization — lives in Mathlib's `Polynomial`/`MvPolynomial` types, where those become theorems to prove rather than `ring` identities.

And the geometry ties it together: a binary quadratic that factors into two **linear** forms is a **degenerate conic** — `x² − y² = (x−y)(x+y)` is the pair of lines `x = y` and `x = −y`.
