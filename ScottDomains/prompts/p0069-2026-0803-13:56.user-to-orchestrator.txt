How hard is it to factor homoenous polynomials

## turn — assistant 2026-08-03T13:57:18.239000-07:00

A wonderful question, Dr. Scott — and the answer is a genuinely pleasant surprise: **computationally, it's *easy* — polynomial time.** That's worth savoring, because the sibling problem, *integer* factorization, is believed hard (no polynomial algorithm known, and much of cryptography rests on that). Polynomials factor in polynomial time; integers, apparently, do not.

Let me be precise, by number of variables.

## Binary forms (two variables) — as easy as univariate

A binary form `F(x,y)` of degree `d` is *equivalent* to a one-variable polynomial. **Dehomogenize** — set `y = 1`, get `f(x) = F(x,1)` of degree ≤ d — factor that, then **re-homogenize** each factor (and restore any factor of `y` you lost). So factoring binary forms **≡ univariate factorization**, and:

- Over **ℚ**: polynomial time, by **Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL, 1982)** — lattice basis reduction turned univariate rational factoring from "hard-looking" into provably efficient.
- Over a **finite field** 𝔽_q: polynomial time (randomized) via **Berlekamp** / **Cantor–Zassenhaus**.

Geometrically — your setting — a binary form of degree `d` is exactly **`d` points on the projective line ℙ¹** (its roots with multiplicity). Over an algebraically closed field it splits *completely* into `d` linear factors. Your degenerate conic `x² − y² = (x−y)(x+y)` is just "the binary quadratic has two roots on ℙ¹," i.e. the pair of lines.

## Ternary and higher forms — still polynomial time

A homogeneous polynomial in `n` variables dehomogenizes to a general polynomial in `n−1` variables. Multivariate factorization over ℚ (or 𝔽_q) is **also polynomial time** — **Kaltofen (1980s)**, via evaluation to the univariate case plus **Hensel lifting** to recover the multivariate factors. Homogeneity is a mild *convenience* (it constrains the shapes of the factors — each factor is itself homogeneous), not a change in complexity class.

## Where the difficulty actually lives

The "hardness" is in the constants and the field, not the complexity class:

1. **The base field decides the answer's shape.** Over ℂ everything splits into linear forms; over ℝ you get linear and irreducible-quadratic forms; over ℚ higher-degree irreducibles persist, and factor coefficients may live in extensions. Same *algorithm*, very different *output*.
2. **Coefficient growth.** LLL and Hensel lifting are polynomial, but intermediate coefficients (heights) can swell, so a large-height form is slow in practice even though it's poly-time in theory.
3. **Verify vs. discover** — the gap you saw in the Lean file. *Verifying* a factorization is trivial (multiply out — `ring`). *Discovering* it is the algorithmic work above. Both are easy in the complexity sense; only the second is interesting.

## The formalization angle

So the *algorithm* is easy — but a **certified** factoring procedure is a different matter. Mathlib has the *theory* — polynomials over a field form a unique factorization domain, irreducibility, etc. — but there's no standard *tactic* that hands you the factors *with a kernel-checked proof* the way `ring` verifies a guess. That gap is engineering, not complexity: producing the factors is fast; producing them *together with a formal certificate* is the work no one has fully packaged.
